The Weighbridge

Do you declare your load light, or heavy?

Every stat on the profile today asks how well you bid. None asks how high. BID ACCURACY is unsigned by construction — a player who is chronically a level under the table and a player who is chronically a level over score identically, because gradeFromProbs only reads the probability the model put on the call you actually made, never which direction you missed in. This board proposes the missing axis, and three ways to draw it.

A weighbridge is the platform scale at a toll house: a wagon is weighed before it crosses, and the toll is set by the load. You declare your load when you bid. The weighbridge says whether you declared it light.

The measurement

One number per call, in levels

Every human call already stores the model's full masked policy over the legal actions — BidEvaluation.probs, 38 floats, softmaxed over legal calls only and renormalised (model.ts's policy()). It is written to boards.bid_evals on every call and has never been read by anything but the call inspector.

That distribution is the baseline. Give every legal call a height — the level it commits the auction to — and the model's expected height is the load the table expected you to declare. Your own call's height, minus that, is the lean: negative light, positive heavy, in levels.

The definition

h₀ = level of the last bid so far (0 if none) height(a) = isBid(a) ? bidLevel(a) : h₀ assessed = Σ probs[a] · height(a) lean = height(call) − assessed

Three properties fall out of that definition, and each one is a thing a cruder version gets wrong.

A strain disagreement scores zero

3NT where the model wanted 3♣ is a shape opinion, not a nerve one — both are height 3, so the lean is ~0. Comparing your call to the argmax alone would have charged you a full step for choosing a different denomination at the same height.

Doubles fall out of this axis

X and XX add no height, so doubling where the model passes reads 0.00 here. That is correct: the axe is a separate temperament, and it gets its own small reading rather than polluting this one.

The baseline already knows the table

encode.ts puts vulnerability, seat and the whole auction into the observation, so "expected height" is situational, not a constant. Favourable vulnerability licenses more, and the baseline already believes that — which is exactly what a naïve aggression figure gets wrong.

For

  • One glanceable figure, in the tile rhythm the page already has.
  • Drops into Compare for nothing: it is a mean of a continuous score, so σ/√n is its error model — the same one bid accuracy already uses — and compare.ts is a pure function of two PlayerStats. Two players with identical accuracy and opposite nerve is the most interesting row that screen could draw.
  • Reuses ds/BeamBar unchanged. No new component.

Against

  • A scalar hides where the lean lives. A player who is a level light in competition and half a level heavy at their own auctions averages to zero and is told they are even-handed — the one reading that is certainly false.
  • Says nothing actionable. "You bid light" is a horoscope until it is paired with what the cards returned.
II

The Load Table

A shape, not a score

Refuse the scalar. Bucket every weighed call by its lean and draw the distribution — the profile's existing TRICKS MADE stem chart, pointed at a different quantity, with the unclamped mean as its dashed marker. Temperament is a spread, and a player who is wild in both directions is a different player from one who is quietly, consistently light.

For

  • Honest about spread, which the mean throws away. A flat, wide distribution and a tight one centred in the same place are different players.
  • Byte-for-byte the idiom already on the page (trickDelta → ds/StemChart), so it costs no new component and reads as native on first sight.

Against

  • The centre bucket dominates. Most calls in most auctions are agreed, so the chart is mostly one spike and a reader has to work to see the skew that is the actual finding.
  • No figure to hand the tiles row, the Compare beam, or a percentile. A distribution cannot be ranked.

For

  • It is a finding, not a horoscope. Light plus overtricks means the caution is costing tricks you were always going to take. Light plus going down means the caution is not the leak — the play is, and the panel should say so instead of nagging about the bidding.
  • The contested split is where the leak actually lives, and it is the split the literature keeps pointing at: letting the opponents buy the contract cheaply is the classic modern leak, and it is invisible in an all-calls average.
  • Keeps I's virtues — the headline percentile still ranks, each row still gets a gated beam, and the Compare row is still free.

Against

  • Two figures with different denominators — calls against declared boards. The copy has to keep them apart, or it reads as one ratio.
  • The carried column is play-confounded — overtricks are partly declarer skill, not only a light auction — and the adage behind it is an IMP-and-rubber argument about game decisions, not a matchpoint one. Corroboration, never proof, and worded as such. See the precedent section below.
  • Needs one small new classifier — has the opposition bid? — since bidCategory buckets every pass together regardless of whose auction it is, and a pass in a contested auction is the single most conservative act available.
The precedent

What is borrowed, and what is not

Strip the toll house off this board and most of it turns out to be established bridge practice wearing new words. One piece is not, and it is worth being straight about which.

Borrowed, and long established

  1. The question is already on the convention card. The standard ACBL card has boxes for light, normal or sound preempt style, a "very light" marker for one-level overcalls, and a light-versus-sound distinction on openings. Per-player bidding conservatism is not an axis this board invented — it is a declared attribute on the disclosure form every pair fills in before play. What is proposed is measuring the thing the card asks you to self-report.
  2. Scoring a call against a distribution of expert opinion is the oldest bidding contest there is. The Bridge World's Master Solvers' Club scores each candidate call out of 100, the director "guided by the votes and comments of a panel of experts," and prints the panel's vote split beside every answer; BBO's Bidders Challenge runs the same format at scale. The stored policy is a machine stand-in for that vote split — which means BID ACCURACY already is an MSC-style score. What is new here is reading the direction of the split, which MSC prints on every problem and has never aggregated across a solver's record.
  3. Comparing the contract to double-dummy par is textbook. "Bid to par, better than par, worse than par" is the standard enumeration; the par contract is a defined term with its own literature, and the method is formalised in a granted US patent for rating bidding performance. This is phase two — and it is the most established idea on the board, so the ordering deserves saying plainly: phase one goes first because it is free and retroactive, not because it is the better measure.
  4. Splitting on whether the opponents have bid is a recognised fault line. The contested auction is its own discipline with its own textbooks; the Law of Total Tricks exists specifically to answer how high to compete; "failing to balance loses more matchpoints than any other reason" is mainstream coaching. Honest caveat: that is consensus and simulation, not a measured error-rate study — I found none.
  5. And bidding is where the variance is. Across 252,434 deals from 932 elite tournaments, deals where the two tables reached different contracts swung 5.88 IMPs on average; deals with the same contract and different card play swung 1.64. That is the argument for putting a bidding statistic on the profile at all.

Not borrowed — no precedent found

  1. The measure itself. A probability-weighted expected contract height, differenced against the height actually bid, does not appear in the bridge literature or in any bridge product I could find. Funbridge reports attack-and-defence splits, contract types, success rates and a per-deal comparison against the optimal line; neither it nor BBO nor Shark Bridge publishes a signed bidding-tendency figure. The nearest precedent is cross-domain: in chess, average centipawn loss is the direct ancestor of bid accuracy — engine-relative, unsigned, aggregated over a career — while Lichess Insights derives further engine-relative traits and ChessBase's Style Report derives an aggressiveness score from positional features. Engine-relative style trait, shipped in a consumer game app is well precedented. It just has not been done in bridge.

Borrowed, but weaker than it first looked

  1. Overtricks as corroboration of a light auction. The adage is real and documented — bidding for the maximum means you should fail to make your contract about as often as you exceed it, and if you never fail you are not aiming high enough. But it is at its strongest at IMPs and rubber, and about game and slam decisions. This is a matchpoint app, where an overtrick in a partscore is itself worth matchpoints and the arithmetic of bidding one more is a different sum. Treatment III's CARRIED column should stay corroboration and be worded as corroboration; it must not be the column the finding rests on.
The honest limits

What it cannot say

  1. The zero point is the model's, not the truth's. The baseline is the SL policy — an imitation learner with a SAYC style of its own. If it is systematically sound or systematically pushy against expert humans, every player's absolute figure shifts with it. The fix is the same one the page already uses: lead with the percentile against the field, computed in fieldPercentiles's existing sweep. Everyone is measured against the same model, so the ranking survives any constant bias the absolute figure does not. Print the raw lean beside it, and say on screen what it is relative to.
  2. A lean inside its own error is not a lean. Report σ/√n over the per-call leans and refuse to name a direction below GATE_SIGMA — Compare's discipline, applied here. Below the gate the panel says even-handed so far rather than inventing a temperament from nine calls.
  3. Describe the result, never the player. Pavlicek's bidding-judgment material is deliberately written in terms of the bid — overbid, underbid — and never the person. "You declared half a level light in contested auctions" is a measurement. "You are a conservative bidder" is a label the player did not ask for and cannot argue with.
  4. This is a matchpoint app. The correct amount of aggression is scoring-dependent, and every board here is matchpoints. Nothing needs to change today, but the figure should not be quoted as a general bridge virtue.
  5. One thing to verify before building. probs is required on BidEvaluation and written on every call, but web/src/api.ts already carries "absent on old boards" comments for two later fields — so the oldest rows deserve a check through the outreach skill's read-only path. If any row lacks it, the fallback is exact and needs no new data: height(call) − height(bestCall), using the stored argmax. Coarser, never wrong.

Phase two — the same question, asked of the cards

Everything above is measured against the table's expectation. The stronger version measures against the deal: was the contract you bought above, at, or below double-dummy par — stopped short, on par, or overboard. That is the established analysis, and the machinery is already here (dealerParFor, ddTableTricks, wired up in analyze.ts's computePar).

It is deliberately not phase one. Par is the slowest DDS call in the app and runs only when someone opens Analyze with ?par=1, so the only boards that carry it are the boards a player chose to review — which is exactly the sample you cannot build a career statistic from. It becomes available the day board_analyses.par coverage is near-total or a backfill is run, and then the two sit side by side and say different things: what the table expected of you, against what the cards would have allowed.

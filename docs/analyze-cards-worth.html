What were the cards worth?

The overview's second panel answers the question every post-mortem starts with — was this board winnable? — and right now it answers in shorthand. Three readings live in one undifferentiated block: par (what perfect bidding and play would have settled on), your table (what actually happened, which today only appears up in the header), and the field (what the other tables did). The proposals below untangle those three, each a different distance from the current implementation.

THE CARDS WERE WORTH

3D*-EW-1 — +100.

Par is played with all four hands face up. Nobody bids that way.

The field here: 2♥ · 1NT ×2 · 3NT.

What today's panel makes the reader decode
  • 3D*-EW-1 is solver notation, not English. Contract, doubling, declaring side and result are fused into one token nobody has met elsewhere in the app — every other surface says "3♦ doubled by East–West, one down."
  • The score's sign is unexplained. +100 is worth to your side, but nothing says so — and on a board you went minus, an unexplained plus reads as a contradiction.
  • The field row names contracts without results. "1NT ×2" hides exactly the thing matchpoints care about: two tables in the same contract usually scored differently. And nothing marks which table was yours.
  • No delineation. Par, caveat and field sit in one run of similar type, so the three different claims blur into one.

All three proposals below draw the same specimen board — yours from the preview: 3NT by W +2 · −460 · 0% in a field of four, with par 3NT by West made exactly, −400 to your side. Frames at 390 reference width, design-system pieces only, copy proposed final.

Proposal A

The Assessor's Lines

Keep the single panel, but give each of the three claims its own labelled ledger line with its own explainer — the settings-gate pattern (label · value · italic aside), applied three times. Par becomes plain words plus one big number whose direction is stated; your table joins the panel so the comparison doesn't depend on the header; the field becomes a per-table column with results, percentages, and a YOU row.

Why this works

Nothing needs decoding: every number carries its direction and its sentence. The field table finally shows results, which is what separates four tables in the same contract. Cheapest to build — it is the current panel re-set, plus field rows the server already sends for the Result's field view.

The trade

Tallest of the three, and it stays prose-first — the par-vs-you-vs-field distances are stated, not seen.

Proposal B

The Gate Line

Put every table's score on one rail, with par as the dashed gate — the Compare screen's gate motif, reused where it means the same thing. One glance answers how far each table sat from what the cards allowed, and where you sit among them. The prose shrinks to one caveat line.

Why this works

The three claims become one picture: par is a place, the field is a scatter, you are the filled dot. Distances do the explaining — "sixty points past par" is visible before it is read. Reuses the gate's meaning from Compare, so the vocabulary is already taught.

The trade

A real score axis has real problems: a −1100 sacrifice next to a +140 partial squeezes everything else into a corner, so the axis needs compression rules, collision handling for stacked labels, and its own reduced-motion-style fallback list. The most new machinery of the three.

Proposal C

The Two Receipts

Par and your table as paired receipts — the toll office's own move. The par stub is printed on the dashed "sealed" border (a receipt for a crossing nobody made); yours is a real one. The field folds to a strip of result chips underneath, yours marked.

Why this works

The most brand-native reading: par as the receipt the toll office would have printed, beside the one it did. The two-up layout makes the par-vs-you comparison the composition itself, and the chip strip keeps the field compact.

The trade

Two stubs sharing 390px leaves each about 170px, so long contract names and the explainers run tight; the chip strip repeats the field-token problem at small size (results present now, but dense). New styles for the stub pair and chips.

Proposal D

The Receipt and the Rail

C and B keep each other's weaknesses out. C's weakest element is its chip strip — the field compressed back into tokens; B's weakest is carrying everything on the rail, so every dot needs a full label and the panel needs a number up top. So: C's paired receipts answer "what were the cards worth, and what did I pay" in plain words with real numbers, and B's rail replaces the chip strip to answer only the one thing a picture answers best — where the field landed around the gate. Because the receipts already carry par and your score, the rail's labelling drops to score-plus-contract ticks and a bare PAR flag: no big number, no caption doing arithmetic.

Why this works

Each device does the one job it is best at: the receipts state the two numbers that need words, the rail shows the one relationship words state badly. Nothing appears twice, so combining them doesn't compound — D is C minus chips plus a lighter B, and lands about as tall as A.

The trade

The rail keeps B's real obligations at reduced stakes: axis compression for a wild score (a −1100 sacrifice), alternating labels for near-ties, and a degenerate-board fallback — which here is simply C (receipts + chips), so the fallback is a design you already like rather than a new state.

Recommendation: D. It is genuinely the best of B and C rather than their sum, because the two halves stop overlapping: the receipts carry every number that needs a sentence, so the rail sheds the labelling that made B heavy, and the rail replaces the chip strip that was C's weakest row. Its honest cost is B's axis rules at reduced stakes — and its degenerate fallback is exactly C, so the safety net is a design already liked rather than a new state to design. If the axis rules feel like too much for this round, C ships as drawn and D remains a pure addition later (the rail slots where the chips sit).

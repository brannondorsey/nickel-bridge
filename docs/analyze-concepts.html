Walking it back

Status — direction chosen · Aug 3, 2026
Concept B, The Second Crossing, with the EXCUSED stamp for set-aside moments and NEXT BOARD keeping the Result’s primary slot (WALK IT BACK → as secondary). The exploration below is kept as the design record; the closing recommendation was not taken — the owner chose the full replay.

The Tournament ledger has promised “Tap a board to look back over the play” since the redesign, and the tap has never kept the promise. Analyze is that missing surface: a finished board, walked again, with the app saying where it turned and what the alternatives were worth. The verdict machinery is settled by the spec and is not re-litigated here — two engines (double-dummy for cost, sampled-DD for findability), cost measured in matchpoints against the real field, a floor (MOMENT_FLOOR) under what gets said, and the claim tail off limits. What the spec leaves open is the part a player actually touches: how the reading arrives.

The three concepts below differ on exactly that axis — read it as one printed audit, walk it as a second crossing of the live board, or jump to it as turning points surfaced on the score itself. They share entries, verdicts, copy register, and refusing states; each could ship alone, and the closing table weighs them.

Concept A

The Auditor’s Ledger

read it — one printed audit, no machinery

Result · WALK IT BACK → · one scrolling audit at /t/:tid/b/:no/analyze — lens switch filters the page · moment rows jump-scroll to their trick · back returns to the Result

The whole analysis is a single printed document, in the register the app already reads best: a perforated ledger at the top saying where it turned, the auction deepened below it, and the play as a trick-by-trick table — every card of all thirteen tricks, DD marks in the margin, the two moments expanded in place with their asides. Nothing animates; nothing is hidden behind a step-through. It is the toll office’s audit of your crossing, stamped and filed. Cheapest to build by a distance — no replay driver, no rewind semantics, and it is the reduced-motion rendering the other concepts must carry anyway — and every moment is a shareable anchor on a URL. The cost: nobody watches a card land, and thirteen rows of card notation ask more bridge literacy than a beginner has — the asides carry them, but the table between the asides is a wall a newer player reads past rather than through.

Concept B

The Second Crossing

walk it — the live board, replayed under a caption ribbon

Result · WALK IT BACK → · ledger hub → tap a moment → the board replays from that trick, four hands open · step or scrub · ‹ back to the hub

The spec’s fullest shape: the moments ledger is a hub, and each row opens the real board — AuctionGrid, TrickArea, HandFan, the same staged glides as live play — stepping trick by trick with an audit ribbon narrating over it. This is the only concept where a beginner watches the hand happen again, which is the form they can actually read; card notation never has to carry the story. It is also the most build by a distance: a replay driver extracted from the tour (which already proves the shape works), rewind semantics TrickArea has never needed, a new replay dock, and the multi-trick stager for jumps. The ribbon is the tour’s narration surface re-registered — unvoiced, no tollkeeper; the audit speaks in the app’s own quiet second person.

B4 · The claim tail, off limits

Past the claim boundary the pips go dashed, the ribbon says why once, and the tail replays at the claim’s own compressed pacing with no marks and no grades — the server played these cards, not you, and grading them would be a false statement. The same boundary drawn the same way in every concept; only B has to draw it mid-motion.

Concept C

Where It Turned, In Place

jump to it — turning points on the score itself, no new room

Result is the surface · moments panel under the hero → tap a moment → bottom sheet with the frozen position · ‹ › pages between moments · no new route

Chess.com’s Key Moments, filed where the score already lives: the Result gains the WHERE IT TURNED panel, and each moment opens a bottom sheet — the app’s existing inspector furniture — holding the frozen position, the card you played beside the card that was findable, and the cost as a two-line toll receipt. No lens switch, no separate screen, no replay: the auction’s deepening happens in the YOUR BIDDING rows the Result already has, which become tappable where a counterfactual exists. One tap from the score to the answer, and a board where nothing turned costs the screen a single quiet line. The trade is scope: nobody ever re-watches the play, the frozen diagram asks notation literacy at the exact moment a replay would have shown the story, and the Result — already the app’s densest screen — takes on more weight.

Weighing them

Comparison & recommendation

ConceptThe reading arrives asAnswers “where did it turn” inMachinery beyond the shared verdict pipelineSharpest edge / dullest edge
A · The Auditor’s Ledger one printed, scrolling audit — read one screen, zero taps two new panels and a trick table; no replay driver, no rewind. It is the reduced-motion rendering B needs anyway. Cheapest, calmest, shareable to the anchor / thirteen rows of card notation between the asides — the beginner reads past the wall.
B · The Second Crossing the live board, replayed under a ribbon — watch one tap from the hub, then motion useReplay extracted from the tour, Result exported with an actions slot, a replay dock, rewind + multi-trick staging TrickArea has never done. The only form a beginner can truly read, and the spec’s promise kept literally / the most build, and the spec’s own success measure admits the replay may be over-built.
C · Where It Turned, In Place turning points on the Result, each a bottom sheet — jump zero navigation; it’s on the score a moment sheet on existing Dialog furniture and Result surgery; no new route, no lens switch, no replay. Shortest path from “25%” to “why” in the whole design space / the play is never re-watchable, and the Result carries yet more weight.
Recommendation

A as the spine, wearing C’s best move. Ship the Auditor’s Ledger — route, lens switch, refusing states, and the verdict pipeline it shares with everything else — but let its moment rows expand into C’s treatment: the frozen position, the two cards, the cost receipt, in place in the scroll (or as the sheet, on the same data). That composite answers the beginner with the two-cards picture instead of the notation wall, keeps the audit shareable and motionless, and builds nothing that later work throws away.

B is a phase, not a casualty. A’s page is byte-for-byte the reduced-motion rendering B must carry, and its ledger is B’s hub — so the replay mounts behind the same rows later, if A’s asides prove not to be enough. The spec’s own success measure (“share of opened analyses that reach the replay”) then gets decided by usage rather than argued in advance. What would change my recommendation: if the tour’s replay machinery extracts more cleanly than estimated, B’s marginal cost shrinks and “walk it” could lead — worth one timeboxed spike on useReplay before committing.

Two decisions wanted with the direction: the verdict vocabulary — stars everywhere (one scale, arguably over-claiming commensurability) versus the EXCUSED stamp for set-aside moments (drawn in A1, B3, C3; a stamp rules for you where a grade rates you) — and which action leads the Result, WALK IT BACK or NEXT BOARD (drawn in E1).

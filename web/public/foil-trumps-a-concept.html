§ 1

What changed

The bleed is fixed, and it was not a tuning problem. Round one painted every card from one canvas pinned to the viewport. A viewport-fixed layer is composited on its own thread, so during a scroll the browser moves the cards immediately and repaints the foil only on the next frame — the colour visibly slid off the cards and onto the felt. No amount of redrawing faster fixes that, because the two layers are not moving together. The foil layer now lives inside each fan and each trick, so it scrolls with the cards natively and there is nothing left to lag.

The hologram is quieter. Saturation is down from 0.62 to 0.34, the polychrome field's spatial frequency is down by more than half, and each reading now sits on a pale base wash rather than on bare paper — so it reads as tinted stock that happens to be iridescent, instead of as a rainbow.

The grating keeps its lines and changes its reflection. The ruled diffraction is untouched; what varies across the three is the shape of the light that ignites them — a travelling band, a pool, or a hard blade.

And the light answers to the device. §4 has the accelerometer, in two models — and only one of them behaves like a real card.

§ 3

The table

Spades are trumps. Five in hand, one on the table where South ruffed the diamond lead — so the same reading can be judged on a card lying flat and on cards held in a curve at once.

Dragging the felt drives whatever DRIVES is set to: with THE LAMP it places the light, which then stays where you put it; with THE HAND it tilts the cards, which is the thumb-driven stand-in for the gyroscope. Until the lamp is placed by hand it drifts over the table on its own, so nothing here is ever sitting dark.

§ 4

The tilt

Your idea, and it turns out there are two ways to build it that feel completely different. Set INPUT to DEVICE and switch DRIVES between the two. Everything on this page answers it — the table, the reference strips and all 123 tiles of the contact sheet, which take the same tilt so the grid stays a fair comparison.

On this page, on a phone, it will almost certainly do nothing — and that is not your phone. An artifact renders inside a cross-origin frame, and the motion sensors are gated by Permissions Policy: gyroscope and accelerometer default to the top-level page only, so a framed document gets no events unless the host frame opts it in. iOS is stricter still — Safari refuses requestPermission() from a cross-origin frame outright ("Source frame did not have the same security origin as the main page"), and WebKit has a standing bug where third-party frames get no motion events even when the policy does allow them. Nothing inside this document can grant itself the sensor.

So the board says which of those it is, live, on your device — rather than sitting there quietly doing nothing:

The stand-in that does work on a phone: drag on the felt. With DRIVES set to THE HAND, dragging a thumb across the table now rotates the cards exactly as the gyroscope would — same code path, same uniforms, a finger instead of a sensor. It is enough to judge whether the motion is right. To feel the real thing the board has to be served from the app's own origin over HTTPS, which is a preview deploy, not an artifact.

The lamp

Tilting moves the light around the room while the cards stay put. It is the easy build and it is subtly wrong: the whole hand brightens and dims together, because every card keeps facing you and only the distance to the lamp changes. It reads like a torch being waved at a photograph.

The hand

The lamp is nailed to the room and tilting rotates the cards — every surface normal turns together, on top of the curvature each card already has from its place in the fan. Now the shine crosses the hand as you twist, one card catches it before its neighbour, and holding the phone still holds the highlight still. This is the one that behaves like a real card, and it is the default.

The rest pose

The first reading is a calibration, not just a zero. It sets the flat pose and retires every self-demo orbit on the page, putting the lamp square over each hand and far enough back that every card sees it from nearly the same angle — the placement that maximises the dimmest card rather than the brightest. So the phone at rest is the well-lit pose, and tilting redistributes the highlight instead of hunting for it. Fan curvature came down to 0.38 to make that literally true: a card lights within about 0.485 rad of the lamp and its angle is at most curve × 0.95, so above ~0.5 the ends of the fan sit dark wherever you put the light. Add ?device=1 to see that pose on a desktop.

Smoothing

Raw deviceorientation is unusable here and it is worth saying why: the shine is a derivative of the angle, so hand tremor and sensor noise you cannot see in the phone's position are plainly visible in the light. The reading now feeds a target and the frame loop eases toward it on a 190 ms time constant — expressed as time rather than a per-frame blend, so it filters identically at 30, 60 and 120 Hz instead of twice as hard on a fast display. The cost is about a fifth of a second of lag, which nobody notices, against flicker everybody does.

On a desktop

POINTER feeds the same two models from the mouse, so the whole idea is judgeable without picking up a phone. It is a stand-in for review, not a shipping input — a board you have to hover to light is a board that is dark whenever you are reading it.

Downloading it

Saving this page and opening it from Files will not help, and it is worth knowing why rather than trying it: a file:// document has a null origin, and requestPermission() is specified to need a secure context plus a real user gesture. Null origin fails the first test, so iOS refuses before the prompt can appear. Android's Chrome does treat file:// as trustworthy, so it may work there — but the only surface that works everywhere is the app's own origin over HTTPS, top-level, which is a preview deploy.

What it costs

deviceorientation needs a user gesture and an explicit grant on iOS, so the setting has to survive being refused: no grant means the hand falls back to the fixed curvature and still foils, just without the twist — which is exactly what this page is doing right now. Absolute orientation also drifts and means nothing indoors, so the first reading is taken as the rest position and everything is reported relative to it: the hand is flat wherever you happen to be holding the phone. In the app this is same-origin and none of the frame problem applies.

§ 5

The hologram — three washes

Same engine as before — polychrome.fs's HSL hue field, seeded per card, which is the part that made you like it. What is different is that the rainbow is now a tint on a base rather than the base itself. The wash is what the eye lands on; the iridescence is what it notices second.

Round one for reference, unchanged: saturation 0.62, full-frequency field, no base. Every strip below is the same maths at saturation 0.34 and a little under half the frequency.

A · i

Blue wash

Pale stamp-blue stock, iridescing.

The base is a pale blue close to the colourblind palette's stamp-ink, so the card reads as cool paper first. It is the calmest of the three and the one that survives being next to a red suit without arguing with it.

The wash sits under the corner index too, which is the only place any of these three costs legibility: a black ♠ pip on faint blue is fine, a red ♥ on it is worse.

A · ii

Rose wash

Warm, and closer to the paper the rest of the app is printed on.

The same field over a pale rose. Warmer stock sits more naturally against --paper than the blue does, so this is the one that looks least like a visitor from another app — but it is also the one nearest the ♥ and ♦ inks, so a heart-suit trump contract would be a foiled red card on a red-ish wash.

A · iii

Duotone

Hue clamped to the cyan–magenta arc — what holographic film actually does.

Real holographic film is not a full spectrum; it is dominated by a cyan-to-magenta sweep with the greens and yellows largely absent. This clamps the hue term to that arc and lets it drift over a lilac base.

It is the most convincing of the three as an object, and the most colourful of the three on screen — which are the two halves of the same decision.

§ 6

The grating — three reflections

The ruled diffraction from holo.fs runs in all three, drawn in page space so the lines carry straight across the whole hand. What changes between the readings is the shape of the light that ignites them.

Two of the rig's sliders now govern the rules themselves. LINE WEIGHT goes from hairline to fat band; LINE COUNT from a handful across the hand to a dense screen. They fall out of the shaping term rather than being bolted on: holo.fs draws a rule as k·|cos| − (k−1), which is non-zero only where the cosine clears (k−1)/k — and whose peak is 1 whatever k is. So k widens or narrows the rule without changing how much light reaches the card, which is exactly what a weight knob should do, and the reason this needed no new maths.

The two levers move all three readings, so you can see what fat rules do to a sweep and a pool as well. The dashed round-one strips stay deaf to them — a reference that moves is not a reference.

Round one for reference: a broad specular lobe, which is the softest and vaguest shape available and the reason the whole card glowed rather than a part of it.

B · i

The sweep

A narrow band that crosses the hand. Ruled lines, three angles.

The specular lobe is squeezed to a narrow travelling band — a gaussian on the specular term rather than a soft ramp — so at any moment two or three cards are lit and the rest are plain. Tilt the phone and the band walks the fan.

This is the closest to holding a real foil card under a ceiling light, and the one I would put money on.

B · ii

The pool

A spill of light on the table. Concentric rules, struck from the lamp.

Distance from the lamp does the gating instead of angle, so the cards nearest the light blaze and the far end of the hand goes dark. The rules are concentric here rather than straight — struck from the lamp, so the geometry agrees with the light rather than cutting across it.

The one shape that reads more like the table is lit than like the cards are. Lovely on the trick, weaker in the hand.

B · iii

The blade

One hard edge, high contrast. A single dense set of rules.

A step rather than a ramp, with a thin overshoot right at the boundary: mostly plain paper, then a bright edge, then plain paper again. The rules are a single set at one angle, so what catches reads as a machined surface rather than a printed one.

This one carries its own bias on top of the sliders — half the k, so a distinctly fatter rule, against a base frequency cut from thirteen to five and a half, so far fewer of them. That is the reading as asked for; LINE WEIGHT and LINE COUNT move it from there in both directions, and at the fat end the blade stops being ruled at all and becomes one broad bar of light crossing the hand.

The most dramatic and the most fragile — a hard edge on fine lines is exactly what aliases on a phone — though fattening the rules is itself most of the cure, since a wide band has far less to alias against. It still wants a derivative-based width at the fine end before it could ship.

§ 7

The contact sheet

Every reading at every setting, laid out so it can be judged by scrolling rather than by moving a slider and trying to remember what the last one looked like. One card per tile, full face, same lamp, same angle, same clock in all of them — the only thing that differs between two tiles is the axis they sit on, which is the whole discipline of a contact sheet.

A true cross-product of every control would be tens of thousands of tiles and useless, so each group varies the two dials that actually move it and inherits the rest from the rig above. Change INTENSITY or CARD STOCK up there and the whole sheet follows, which is the cheapest way to see all of this at night.

Tiles render only while they are on screen, so the sheet costs about what one strip does however far it runs. Hover a tile for its exact numbers.

§ 8

The rule

Unchanged from round one, and still the half that has to be exactly right: a card that shines when it is not trumps is worse than no feature at all.

Which cards

Every card of the trump suit, wherever a card face is drawn: your fan, a tabled dummy fan, the trick. Trump comes from trumpForDisplay — the one place the app already converts a strain to a suit — so foil and the trump-left sort can never disagree about which suit it is.

When it starts

At the beat the contract settles, which is the beat "The Draw" already owns: the trumps are singled out and travel to the left of the fan. Landing lit is one event rather than two.

In the hand

A fanned card is about sixty per cent hidden behind the card to its right, so only the exposed sliver carries foil — and because trumps sort left, the slivers are contiguous and the suit reads as one band down the edge of your hand. Tabled and dummy cards get the full face.

Never

On a no-trump contract, during the auction, and on the E/W dummy rail, which prints ranks as text and has no card face to light.

Off by default

A new row on the settings gate beside Trump placement, the switch it is a companion to — and, if the tilt ships, a second segment for whether the device drives it. Purely a client treatment: the server never reads it and nothing about scoring, robots or replay changes either way.

§ 9

What it costs

The scroll

The bug you caught, and what it taught: a position: fixed layer with mix-blend-mode gets its own compositor layer, and an async scroll moves the page under it before script runs. The foil layer has to be a child of the thing it is lighting. That is now one canvas per fan and per trick — which in the real app is two or three elements on the board screen, not a page full.

One context

Nine canvases would be nine WebGL contexts, and browsers evict them at around sixteen. There is exactly one context here, rendering off-screen; each host blits its own region out of it with drawImage. One shader, one field, N cheap copies.

The edges

Foil now runs to the trim. It used to stop a few pixels short on every side, because the mask feathered over a sixteenth of each dimension — 3px across, 5px down, and worse than it sounds in the hand, where a fanned card shows a ~21px sliver and the fade ate a sixth of it. The fade is gone; what bounds the plate now is the card's own outline, a rounded rect measured in pixels against border-radius, holding back three-quarters of one pixel so the hairline still reads against the felt. Pixels rather than uv is the point — proportional insets feather a small card harder than a large one, and these cards are fluid.

The overhang

That exposed a second one: a trick card is taller than the grid row holding it, so it spills past the host the foil layer was pinned to and its foot came back bare. The layer now overhangs its host by 16px on every side, painting neutral grey where there is no card, which hard-light ignores. Spelling out that size is not decoration — a <canvas> is a REPLACED element, so an absolutely positioned one with width: auto silently takes its intrinsic 300×150 drawing buffer and ignores the opposing offset. It reads as the shader having broken rather than as a layout mistake.

The ink

Still the real risk. The card is DOM text under a blended canvas, so anything lit sits over the rank and suit. Every reading here knocks the corner index out of the mask, which is also the honest period answer — a foil plate covers a field, never the type. The hologram's base wash is the one thing that deliberately crosses that line, since a wash with a hole in it is not a wash.

Reduced motion

FROZEN is the fallback, and with the tilt it gets more interesting: a device-driven highlight is not an animation, it is a response. Under prefers-reduced-motion the drift stops but the twist could reasonably stay — worth a decision rather than a default.

Night stock

The night card face is an ink-plate negative and deliberately low-contrast against the panel, so foil there may only add light — and a pale wash added to dark stock is not a tint, it is a bright rectangle. Left alone the washes blew the trumps into pale slabs and undid exactly the contrast the night card is designed for, so group A's night amplitude is damped to 40% while the gratings keep theirs. Switch CARD STOCK to NIGHT and the gratings are plainly the stronger half — they were already made of light, so the dark plate flatters them.

The brand

Both survivors argue with "flat printed paper, no gradients, max ink and minimum colour". Worth saying plainly rather than pretending otherwise: this is a deliberate exception, opt-in, on exactly thirteen cards of one suit.

Open, for you

A wash and a reflection are not mutually exclusive — the duotone base under the sweep's band is a seventh reading nobody has drawn yet. Say the word and it is the next strip.
